Part 1

Musical chairs with 3.7 million players

Berlin's housing market is musical chairs. The music is people arriving. The chairs are flats. And the rule that matters: a chair only gets a new price when somebody stands up.

The music got loud. Between 2011 and 2025 the official population grew by about 375,000, from 3.33 to 3.70 million.0 That's roughly one Bochum moving in.0 In 2025, all of the growth came from migration: Berlin had more deaths than births.

The chairs did not keep up. The Senat's target has long been up to 20,000 new flats a year. Here is what actually got built:

Flats built per year vs the target

Hover a bar.
Completed flats (new builds + conversions), Amt für Statistik Berlin-Brandenburg. Best year: 18,999 (2019). Never 20,000.

Why the drop after 2019? Building got expensive fast. The interest on a mortgage fixed for over 10 years went from 1.27% (Sept 2021) to 3.92% (Nov 2023), and it was still 4.04% in August 2026. Construction prices rose by a third between 2021 and 2025. Building permits for flats fell from 25,052 in 2016 to 9,772 in 2024.0 So the cranes went home.

Meanwhile, empty flats basically ran out. The 2022 census found 1.97% empty, and only 1.2% had been empty for three months or more. Planners consider about 3% healthy, because people need somewhere to move into.0

So the few chairs that do free up go to whoever pays most. That's where the "Berlin rent" you see online comes from: it's not what Berlin pays, it's what the listings ask. And here's the part nobody tells you at the Besichtigung:

Berlin doesn't have one rent. It has two: the one people pay, and the one people are asked to pay.

The people already inside pay about €7.67 per m² on average (census, 2022). The listings ask a median of €15.78 (2025). Same city. Same kind of buildings. Often the same building.0 Let's put both on a map.

Part 2

The lock-in machine

In most cities, rent is one price that moves. In Berlin, it's two prices that drift apart, and the law glues you to the cheaper one.

If you already have a lease, your rent can rise at most 15% in three years, and never above the Mietspiegel, the official table of "local comparative rents".0 The Mietspiegel is built from rents of the last six years, so it lags the market by design. Its 2026 average: €7.71.

If you want a new lease, you pay what the listing says. Median 2025: €15.78.

Now meet the Hoffmanns. Two adults, 85 m², kids moved out in 2012. They'd happily take a 55 m² flat and give their big one to a family. Drag the years:

Stay = 85 m² × the latest Mietspiegel city average. Move = 55 m² × the IBB median asking rent that year. Simplification: a real sitting tenant's rent can sit above or below the average. Turnover: last BBU figure reported up to that year.

See the crossover around 2018? From then on, the smaller flat costs more than the bigger one. In 2025 the 55 m² flat costs about €868 a month cold, the 85 m² one about €613. Moving out of the too-big flat would cost the Hoffmanns roughly €3,000 a year for the privilege of having less space.

So they stay. So does everyone. At BBU member landlords, the share of flats changing tenants fell from 9.5% (2001) to 4.5% (2025), and the average tenancy is now over 22 years. At the state-owned landlords it's 3.8%, down from "around 10%".0 Fewer people moving means fewer listings: 64,483 regular listings in 2012, 40,014 in 2025.0 Fewer listings means more people per listing, which pushes asking rents up, which widens the gap. Which means fewer people move.

Every Berliner who stays put makes the next listing more expensive, and every expensive listing makes another Berliner stay put.

Nobody in this story is doing anything wrong. The Hoffmanns are being rational. That's what makes it a machine and not a villain.0

Part 3

Everything the city tried

To be fair to Berlin: it tried. It tried a lot. It tried things no other German state tried. Here's how it went, one round at a time. Ring the bell.

Notice a pattern? It isn't that nobody did anything. It's that every tool hit a wall: the federal level (Karlsruhe, the BGB), the exemptions (furnished, temporary, new builds), or time (laws that take effect in 2028, construction that takes years). And the biggest own-goal was scored back when the problem was the opposite one: around 2001, Berlin had roughly 100,000 empty flats and was planning demolitions.0

Boss fight: the Schufa

Almost every landlord asks for a Schufa: a score from Germany's private credit bureau. If you just arrived in Germany, you probably don't have much of a file yet. Choose your moves.

The Anmeldung loop

One more level. To register your address (the Anmeldung, required within two weeks of moving in) you need a form from your landlord. To get a landlord, many ask for… a registered address. To get a tax ID, which your employer needs for your payslips, which the landlord needs, you need the Anmeldung. And the tax office says your surname must be on the letterbox.0

The exit: any flat with a lease, even a sublet, gets you a Wohnungsgeberbestätigung. In Berlin, a booked Bürgeramt appointment counts as registering on time.

    So, why?

    Because Berlin has two shortages stacked on top of each other: too few flats were built for the people who came, and the flats that exist are locked by a gap that doubles your rent the moment you move. The few chairs that free up go to the highest bidder, the loopholes go to the furnished listings, and every fix the city tried was either voided by Karlsruhe, riddled with exemptions, or scheduled for 2028.

    The good news, such as it is: the machine is legible. More building lowers the price of the chairs. Narrowing the gap (or the loopholes) unlocks people. Neither is a mystery. Both are slow.
